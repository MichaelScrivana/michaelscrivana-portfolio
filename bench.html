One gesture. Every material.

Up front · AI mood boards

Before I touch charcoal or clay, I run a mood board for each material — fast generative passes to answer what should this feel like? not what is the final image? The boards set light, gesture, volume, palette, and motion grammar. The physical work proves or kills each direction.

00 · Thesis

The bench is where ideas become things.

Every medium I've picked up lives on the same surface — graphite, oil, clay, a screen, a node graph. Materials don't replace each other; they translate what the last one found.

This project is deliberately not client work. It's a personal experiment: take one object I know from the inside out — my own hand in a maker's pinch — and ask what has to stay true when charcoal becomes clay becomes pixels becomes motion becomes a model's guess. The answer I'm looking for isn't a beautiful final frame. It's a legible chain of decisions a reviewer can follow.

Fix the gesture. Let each material add something the last one couldn't.
01 · The spark

Why the hand?

Because it's the one tool that's been on every bench I've ever worked at.

At the Art Students League it held charcoal and measured proportion. In a Michelin kitchen it held a knife and judged heat by feel. On a packaging table it holds a pencil and a scale model. At a keyboard it wires graphs and writes prompts. The hand isn't a subject I chose for drama — it's the constant behind every other medium.

I locked one gesture: a pinch-grip — thumb and index meeting, middle finger curled, tension visible in the tendon at the wrist, weight settled in the heel of the palm. Asymmetric finger spread. Light from the upper left. Those four things had to survive every translation. Everything else was negotiable.

The design question underneath: what is the idea, and what is just the material talking? If the pinch reads in charcoal but disappears in clay, that's a failure of translation, not of craft.

02 · Physical

See it first.

Charcoal before clay. Always. You have to earn the volume by understanding the line.

The fine art mood board was on the wall next to the easel — value range, light direction, gesture. I started with my left hand on the bench, lit from a single window. Graphite first — loose placement, then value structure. The early study from art school (fine-art.html) was the anchor: I'd drawn hands before, but never with a translation test in mind.

Charcoal → clay: I gained volume and edge in three dimensions. I lost some of the line specificity — a knuckle that read cleanly in graphite became a soft merge in clay if I wasn't careful. The decision: model the pinch at small scale, palm-up, so the thumb-index meeting point stays the hero. Portrait-bust scale was a dead end (see below).

03 · Digital

Volume without losing the drawing.

The 3D pass wasn't a scan-and-done. It was an interpretation.

The sculpture and color boards sat open on the second monitor — volume targets and palette temperature while I modeled. I photographed the maquette from four angles and rebuilt the form in 3D — not photogrammetry-for-its-own-sake, but a modeler's reading of what the clay found. The pinch geometry and tendon tension had to survive; surface detail could simplify.

Clay → particles: this is where I had to name the moment abstraction took over. A literal particle shell of the hand looked like a tech demo. The keeper direction inherited the gesture's energy — tension, direction of force, asymmetric spread — without requiring a readable hand at every frame. That's the honest trade: at some point the hand becomes force, and you decide if that's still your idea.

04 · Motion

Time is the next material.

A twenty-second capstone: hand, form, abstraction, return.

The motion mood board was the edit brief — assemble, dissolve, return. The cut runs the chain in order: charcoal still, clay turn, 3D orbit, particle dissolve, and back to a resolved frame. Motion doesn't add new information; it proves the translations connect. If the cut feels like five unrelated pieces, the experiment failed. If it feels like one idea changing clothes, it worked.

05 · AI

Close the loop — after the making.

The mood boards opened the process. These frames close it.

AI showed up twice: up front as directional mood boards per material, and at the end as synthesis — generative frames that ask whether the gesture still reads after every translation. Physical → AI: I fed the charcoal keeper and the 3D hero as reference, with the same constraints the boards established — pinch-grip, upper-left light, charcoal value range, no extra objects, tendon tension visible. I let the model invent surface texture and atmosphere. I did not let it invent a new gesture.

Most rolls drifted. Fingers multiplied. The pinch opened into a wave. Thumbs grew too long. I kept four frames where the geometry held and something unexpected was worth keeping — usually in the light or the skin texture, not the structure.

This is the difference from CreativeLab: there, brand rules are encoded in a graph because the output has to ship. Here, the constraint is aesthetic and the output is exploratory. Same instinct — hold the idea still, move one variable — different stakes.

06 · Reflection

What survived.

The bench keeps growing materials. The gesture is what has to stay.

AWhat stayed fixed.

Pinch geometry, asymmetric finger spread, tendon tension, upper-left light. If those four read, the medium can do almost anything. If they don't, no amount of craft downstream saves it.

BWhat each material gave.

Charcoal: line and value. Clay: volume and edge. 3D: orbit and re-light. Particles: energy without literal form. AI mood boards: fast direction per material. Final generative frames: synthesis and surprise. None of them made the others obsolete.

CWhat I'd push next.

A physical print of the keeper generative frame next to the charcoal original — same wall, two materials. And a tighter loop: change one variable (light only, or gesture only) and re-run the full chain to see if the system is actually parametric or just a one-off story.
